# M5 · The Knight tier: plan and decisions

**Status: draft, waiting for the designer's approval (gate 1). Nothing is built until then.**

M5 follows M4 (sound, the editor, free play, the Pawn tier's mastery set; complete and published 2026-11-17). It
is the second tier of `DESIGN.md` §4: the player's piece becomes a knight, and the Python is functions, then lists
and tuples. This document is the plan for the whole tier, and the roadmap for what follows it. Each sub-milestone
gets its own spec (`docs/M5/M5.<n>.md`) before it's built.

*(Designer, date)* marks a decision. *(Recommended)* marks a recommendation that waits for the designer's answer.
Chapter outlines say what each level trains and its board mechanic, never how to solve it.

## Decided so far *(Designer, 2026-11-17)*

- **The Knight tier comes now.** The findings from playing M4 (the five challenges, the retests) are folded into
  M5.0 whenever they arrive; planning and building don't wait for them.
- **M5 is the whole Knight tier:** the knight as a piece, Chapter 7, Chapter 8, the promotion to bishop and the
  tier's mastery set. From here on, one milestone per tier.
- **The plan goes one tier deep.** M5 in detail; M6 to M9 as a one-page roadmap ("Beyond M5", below), each
  detailed when it starts, with what play-testing has taught by then.
- **Three gates for every sub-milestone:** the designer approves its draft (for a chapter, a spec of every level),
  plays QA1 when it's feature-complete, and QA2 before the merge. The autonomous run that ended M4 is over.

## How M5 runs

- **One branch, PR and three gates per sub-milestone** (`docs/WORKFLOW.md`). Merged branches are kept.
- **A spec first.** Each sub-milestone's spec holds its decisions, design sheets, hand-test list and work
  breakdown. This plan settles what reaches across sub-milestones.
- **Design choices are shown first** (the M3.7 rule): the knight's jump, the squares it can land on, new tiles and
  the bishop skin are tried on dev pages in `design/` and go into the game when the designer says so.
- **Level specs before levels** (the M3 rule): the designer approves concept, board, rules, par and cases for
  every level. References, naive attempts, hints and notes are written blind by the orchestrator's generators.
- **Who builds:** the orchestrator coordinates and writes level content; Opus leads and Sonnet workers build the
  code; usage over speed, one package at a time.

| Sub-milestone | What | Why in this order |
|---|---|---|
| M5.0 | Startup: this plan, the roadmap into `DESIGN.md`, the M4 play-test round when its findings arrive | Clears the decks |
| M5.1 | The knight as a piece: its rules in the engine, `knight.…` in code, its look and motion, the Codex, practice levels in the Testing ground | Everything else in the tier stands on it. The first time the rules change, not only the skin |
| M5.2 | Chapter 7 · Spellbook: `def`, parameters, `return`, scope | Functions first: Chapter 8's levels are written with them |
| M5.3 | Chapter 8 · The Satchel: lists, tuples, iteration, sorting; keys, doors and an inventory | Closes the tier's teaching path |
| M5.4 | The promotion to bishop (ceremony, bishop skin) and the Knight tier's mastery set in Free Play | Needs both chapters |
| M5.5 | The level editor learns the tier: the knight as a level's piece, the new tiles, the solvability check | Once, after every new tile exists (decision 9) |

## What the code already gives us

Checked on `main`, so the plan stands on facts:

- **Pieces are a registry.** `engine/rankfile/pieces.py` has `Piece`, `Pawn` and `PIECES = {"pawn": Pawn}`. A
  level names its piece (`level.piece`), and the runner puts it in the player's namespace under that name. Locked
  abilities, the friendly errors and the Codex entries (the methods' docstrings) all come from the class.
- **`position` is already a tuple,** `(x, y)` with a1 at `(0, 0)`. Chapter 8 can build on what the player has
  been printing since Chapter 2.
- **A tier is worked out by the engine** from a level's abilities, required constructs and piece (`codex.py`).
- **Promotions are a table** (`src/promotion.ts`): pawn to knight is its one row. Skins are `"pawn" | "knight"`.
- **The pawn is assumed in many places:** about 225 mentions across 51 engine and UI files (the board's drawing,
  autocomplete, the editor, motion, the solvability search, tests). Most will be harmless names; finding the ones
  that are real assumptions is M5.1's first job, an investigation by an Opus lead before its spec is written.

## Open decisions

Each has options and a recommendation. A yes to "the recommendations" approves them all; anything else, say
which. Decisions 1 to 4 shape M5.1; 5 to 7, Chapter 7; 8, Chapter 8; 9 to 11, the rest.

### 1. How the knight moves in code

The biggest one: it shapes every level from here to the capstone. The teaser on the promotion screen already
promises "The knight moves in an L, over anything in its way."

| | The call | For | Against |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. By offset** *(Recommended)* | `knight.jump(1, 2)`: one square right, two up. Any of the eight L shapes; anything else is a friendly error ("a knight moves two one way and one the other") | Speaks the language `position` already taught: `(x, y)`. Parameters mean something from the first level of Chapter 7. Chapter 8 gets routes as lists of pairs for free. The bishop, rook and queen can move the same way later. The knight-on-a-chessboard problem every Python course ends with (Chapter 13) uses exactly these pairs | Negative numbers and coordinates: a step up from "forward". That is a fair step for tier 2, and the board can draw the eight squares |
| B. By facing | `knight.leap_left()`, `knight.leap_right()`: two ahead, one to the side; `turn_left()` and `turn_right()` stay | Feels like the pawn. Small | Only two moves per facing, so turning comes back as busywork. Nothing for parameters or tuples to hold on to. Each later piece needs a new scheme |
| C. By square | `knight.jump_to("c3")` | Reads like chess | Invites typing squares off the board by eye. Little arithmetic. Several boards make it awkward |
| D. By compass | `knight.jump("north", "east")`: two north, one east | Friendly words | Text standing in for numbers, easy to mistype, nothing to compute with |

With A, the knight has **no facing**: it doesn't turn. Its sprite looks the way it last jumped.

### 2. What the knight keeps from the pawn

- **A. A pure knight** *(Recommended)*: it only jumps. It keeps what isn't about walking: `position`, `at_goal()`,
  `wait()`, `read()`. It loses `move()`, the turns, `facing`, `squares_ahead()`, the diagonal captures and
  `bridge()`; asking for one says so kindly ("knights don't walk: they jump"). `look()` becomes
  `knight.look(1, 2)`: what is on the square that jump would land on.
- B. A knight that can also walk: the pawn's whole API plus `jump`. Easier boards, but most levels could then be
  solved the old way, and "a piece is its rules" (`DESIGN.md` §8) stops being true.

"A promotion changes what you can do" is the point of the tier, and a walking knight blurs it. Straight lines
then become something the player *builds* out of jumps, which is exactly what Chapter 7 needs functions for.

### 3. What a jump does on the board *(Recommended as a set)*

- It passes **over** anything: walls, pits, gates, enemies. Only the landing square matters.
- Landing on a wall, a signpost, a shut gate or off the board is an error, as bumping is now. Landing in a pit or
  on an attacked square loses the run, as now.
- **Landing on an enemy takes it,** as a chess knight does. No separate capture ability.
- One jump is one action for every clock (patrols, chasers and timed gates move once).
- **The board shows where the knight can land** from where it stands, before the run and while stepping, the way
  attacked squares are drawn now. The look goes on a design sheet in M5.1.
- A principle for every level of the tier: **the puzzle is the Python, never the knight's geometry.** Working
  out a knight's route by eye is a chess puzzle; boards are laid out, or say enough, that the hard part is the
  concept the level trains.

### 4. What the knight looks like on its own levels

Today Settings → Piece is a skin for every level, and the pawn is the default.

- **A. "Auto" becomes the default** *(Recommended)*: each level shows its own piece (pawn on Chapters 1 to 6,
  knight from Chapter 7). Choosing a skin still overrides it everywhere, and still never renames the object in
  code. Players who never open Settings see the right piece.
- B. As now: the pawn skin until the player picks the knight. A knight level could show a pawn that jumps.

The open note from M4.4 that the Charger "looks little like its card" when facing north belongs here: the knight
is now the hero, so its sprite gets its design sheet in M5.1.

### 5. Where the player learns the jump

Chapter 7's five levels are about functions. The jump needs a first level of its own, and Lessons is the teaching
path (the Testing ground is optional).

- **A. Chapter 7 gets a sixth core level** *(Recommended)*: 7.1 teaches the jump, 7.2 to 7.6 teach functions, then
  the optional mastery. Every tier's first chapter would open the same way, with one level for the new piece.
- B. Keep five: the jump shares 7.1 with `def`. Two new things in one level, which the pedagogy rules warn
  against.
- C. A short prologue chapter for the knight (two or three levels, no mastery), as Chapter 1 was for the pawn.
  Chapters 7 on would be renumbered.

### 6. "The level calls your function"

`DESIGN.md` §4 says the player's role shifts in the middle tiers from driving the piece to programming behaviours.
Functions are where that can start: a level names a function the player must write, then **calls it itself**,
once per board or per test, and judges what it did or returned.

- **A. Bring it in with Chapter 7** *(Recommended)*, for the `return` level and the mastery challenge. In the
  story it is an **examiner**: a guard who hands your function a few inputs, shown before the run, and checks
  each answer. It is the first taste of testing (Chapter 16), and the base for Workshop levels later.
- B. Wait for the Bishop tier. Chapter 7 forces `def` with rules and line limits only.

It is new engine work either way (a level format field, the runner calling the function, the result card showing
each call), sized in M5.2's spec. Levels stay data.

### 7. How much of functions Chapter 7 holds

The map says `def`, parameters, `return`, scope. Three neighbours need a home:

- **Default values** (`def hop(times=1)`): *(Recommended)* in Chapter 7, with parameters. The player has used
  them since `pawn.move()` and can now see how they are made.
- **Docstrings:** *(Recommended)* a lesson aside in Chapter 7, no level of its own: `help()` on the player's own
  function shows the text they wrote, exactly as it does for the piece's abilities.
- **Keyword arguments, `*args`, `**kwargs`:** *(Recommended)* not here. Keyword arguments arrive with
  `sorted(key=)` in Chapter 11; the star forms with decorators in Chapter 15.

Not proposed for M5: functions that carry over between levels (a real "spellbook"). Every level stays
self-contained so its rules and par mean something; a spellbook the player imports is a natural reward when
modules arrive (Chapter 16).

### 8. Keys, doors and the satchel

A piece that jumps over walls can't be stopped by a door in a corridor, so the pawn-style gate doesn't carry
over as it is. *(Recommended, to be settled in Chapter 8's level specs:)*

- **Keys** lie on squares. The knight takes one with `knight.pick_up()` while standing on it.
- **`knight.inventory`** is a real Python list of what it carries, so `len()`, `in`, indexing and loops over it
  are the lesson, not a wrapper around it.
- **A locked door** is a square the knight may land on only while carrying its key. Some doors want their keys
  gathered in an order the level states.
- **Abilities that return lists and pairs,** so there is data worth looping over and sorting: for example where
  the keys are, as `(x, y)` pairs, differing between a level's boards.

### 9. When the editor learns the knight

- **A. Once, at the end (M5.5)** *(Recommended)*: the piece choice, the new tiles in the palette, the tier tag,
  and a solvability check that can jump. One pass, after Chapter 8's tiles exist.
- B. As each piece and tile lands (in M5.1 and M5.3). Nothing is ever missing from the editor, but its code is
  opened three times.
- C. Not in M5. The editor stays a Pawn-tier tool for now.

Until M5.5, the editor refuses nothing it accepts today; it just can't make a knight level.

### 10. Practice levels for the knight

*(Recommended)* Yes, as M3.1 did for obstacles: two or three short levels in the Testing ground with M5.1 (the
jump; a jump over a wall and onto an enemy; looking before landing). The designer plays the piece before any
chapter is specified, and Chapter 7's specs are written knowing how it feels.

### 11. The Knight tier's mastery set

*(Recommended)* The same shape as the Pawn tier's: five challenges in Free Play → Challenges, no lesson phase,
each mixing both chapters and the Pawn tier's concepts, difficulties from `try` to `while True:`, built by a
generator. A name is suggested in M5.4's spec. **No reward yet:** rewards wait for the experience pass (see
"Beyond M5"), which by then will have two sets to reward.

## Chapter outlines *(proposed; each chapter's level specs can change them)*

### M5.1 · The knight

- **Engine:** a `Knight` in the registry with the abilities decisions 1 to 3 settle; its errors and their
  translations; its Codex entries.
- **Board and UI:** the knight as a level's own piece (decision 4); the jump's motion and sound; the squares it
  can land on; autocomplete, hover help and the variables panel saying `knight`.
- **Testing ground:** the practice levels of decision 10.
- **First step, before the spec:** an Opus lead's investigation of where the app assumes a pawn, so the work
  breakdown names real files.

### M5.2 · Chapter 7 · Spellbook: `def`, parameters, `return`, scope

- **Forcing:** a manoeuvre needed at several places that a loop can't cover, under a line limit; the same
  manoeuvre at different sizes; answers that differ between boards; the examiner (decision 6).
- **New engine and UI work:**
  - the examiner level format
  - rules that can require a `def`, a call to it, or a `return` (checked with `ast`, as every rule is)
  - stepping into a function: the highlighted line follows the call, and the variables panel shows the function's
    own names apart from the program's
  - error translations: a missing or extra argument, a function that returns nothing where a value was wanted,
    a name that only exists inside a function, calling before defining, a function that calls itself forever
  - Codex entries for `def`, `return` and parameters
- **Levels:**
  1. **The jump:** `knight.jump()`; over walls no pawn could pass.
  2. **A spell of your own:** `def` and calling it; a manoeuvre the route needs again and again.
  3. **Spells that take orders:** parameters and default values; one manoeuvre at different sizes.
  4. **Spells that answer:** `return`; the examiner asks for something the function works out.
  5. **What happens in the spell:** scope; names inside a function, names outside, and what crosses between.
  6. **Spells that cast spells:** functions calling functions; a long route taken apart into small pieces.
  - **Mastery:** a behaviour the level runs on several boards, focused on par.

### M5.3 · Chapter 8 · The Satchel: lists, tuples, iteration, sorting

- **Forcing:** routes and key positions that arrive as data and differ between boards; an order that has to be
  worked out; the satchel's contents asked for by a guard.
- **New engine and UI work:** keys, locked doors, `pick_up()`, `inventory`, the list-returning abilities
  (decision 8); the satchel shown beside the board; their tiles on a design sheet; Codex entries for lists,
  tuples and the list tools taught; error translations (`IndexError`, unpacking the wrong number of values,
  changing a tuple).
- **Levels:**
  1. **A list of jumps:** a list, and `for` over it.
  2. **Pairs:** tuples, unpacking and indexing; a jump worked out from two positions.
  3. **The satchel:** `pick_up()`, `inventory`, `len()` and `in`; doors that want their key.
  4. **Gathering:** building a list with `append`, then using it.
  5. **In order:** `sorted()`, `min()` and `max()`; keys wanted in an order not known ahead.
  - **Mastery:** data gathered, ordered and acted on, across several boards.

### M5.4 · Promotion and the mastery set

- The ceremony's second row: knight to bishop, shown once when Chapter 8's core levels are solved. The bishop
  skin on a design sheet first. The teaser says honestly that the Bishop tier isn't built yet.
- The mastery set of decision 11.

## Hand-test list *(draft; each sub-milestone's spec has its own)*

At the end of M5 a player can:

1. Finish Chapter 6, see the promotion, and open Chapter 7 as a knight without visiting Settings.
2. Jump in every L shape, over walls, onto an enemy; get a kind error for a move no knight makes.
3. Step through a function call and watch its own variables come and go.
4. Fail and then pass an examiner, and see each of its calls on the result card.
5. Pick up keys, see the satchel fill, and open a door.
6. Finish Chapter 8, see the promotion to bishop, and wear the bishop skin.
7. Play the Knight tier's five challenges in Free Play, tagged with their tier and difficulty.
8. Build, test and share a knight level in the editor.
9. Play every Pawn-tier level exactly as before.
