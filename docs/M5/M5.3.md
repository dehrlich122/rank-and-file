# M5.3 · Chapter 8, The Satchel: spec

**Status: approved *(Designer, 2026-11-19: "approved, all ten as recommended")*: gate 1 passed, building.** The
next stop for the designer is the picks from the design sheet; nothing a player sees is built before them.
Branch: `m5-3-chapter-8`, made from `m5-2-chapter-7` while M5.2 is at QA1 (PR #28); QA1 findings on M5.2 are fixed
on its own branch and merged forward into this one.

M5.3 is the Knight tier's second chapter: five core levels and an optional mastery challenge that teach lists and
tuples (making them, going through them, building them up, putting them in order), played as the knight, with
keys, locked doors and a satchel. It builds on `docs/M5/M5.md` (decision 8 and the Chapter 8 outline, *(Designer,
2026-11-17)*), on the knight of M5.1 and on the examiner of M5.2.

*(Recommended)* marks a recommendation that waits for the designer's answer. Levels are described by what they
train and their board mechanic, never by how to solve them.

## What the investigation found *(M5.3-0, an Opus lead, read-only, 2026-11-19)*

Run on the real engine unless marked "read". It ran out of room at 150k tokens with four of its twelve questions
open; the orchestrator answered two of them by looking (the Codex's entries, the rules a level can set), and the
other two are the contract package's first job (below, "How it is built").

**Three faults that are already in the published game.** The first is fixed in M5.3 whatever else is decided.

- **The solvability search has no limit on time or memory.** Its only limit is a count of states, and every state
  in its queue carries a full copy of its route, so one long chain grows without end. That is what filled 139 GB
  over 17 hours on 2026-11-19 (a stuck `pytest`; M5.2-S fixed the one board that set it off, not the cause).
  Knight boards can't set it off today, because the search can't jump yet (M5.5).
- **Two wrong messages.** Using the `None` that `.sort()` or `.append()` hands back gets the advice written for
  runes ("If it came from `read()`, your knight isn't standing on a rune"). `list(5)` gets the advice written for
  a `for` loop. Chapter 8's first slip with `append` would meet the first one.
- **A list comprehension** (Chapter 11's shorthand for building a list) hides every name but its own in the
  variables panel while it runs, and records one step per item. No lesson teaches it before Chapter 11.
  *(Recommended: recorded for M7, not fixed here.)*

**What already works:**

- **Lists and tuples in ordinary code:** a `for` over a list of pairs, `sorted`, `min`, `max` and `len` on pairs,
  a list passed to a function. All record and play back correctly.
- **The variables panel shows a list as it was at each step,** not as it ended: a list that grows over four lines
  shows four different values. Long lists show eight items and then `...`.
- **Rewind needs nothing new.** Every step already carries the whole state of the board, so once the satchel is
  part of that state, step, rewind, replay and "show this board" are right by themselves.
- **A tile close to each new one.** The plank is already picked up on landing and counted (though the count is
  never drawn). The guard's gate is already "locked until": a knight that lands on a locked one gets an error at
  that line, not a lost run.
- **Passing a pair where two numbers are wanted** (`knight.jump(pair)`) already gets a kind message. Assigning to
  `knight.inventory` would be refused by the rule that already protects `knight.position`.
- **A guard can already be answered with a list:** it hears the printed line, and a printed list is a line.
- **The Codex** has `len` and `print`, `range`, `int`, `help`; text methods are entries of their own kind.
- **The rules a level can set** already cover what Chapter 8 needs: a line limit, a limit on numbers written in
  the code, the function rules of M5.2. No new rule is proposed.

**What is missing:**

- **Keys, doors, `pick_up()` and `inventory`** do not exist. A tile kind is named in about twenty places from the
  level file to the sprite; the investigation listed each with its line.
- **The examiner refuses a list** as an argument or as the answer it wants ("must be a plain value"), and a
  lesson snippet the examiner calls takes plain values only.
- **A level can hand code only text** (a rune's words) and the knight's `position`.
- **Raw Python** for every mistake a list invites: an index past the end, unpacking the wrong number of values,
  changing a tuple, sorting text mixed with numbers, `remove` of something that isn't there, `max` of an empty
  list.
- **The Codex** has no `sorted`, `min`, `max` or `sum`, no list methods, and nothing on what a list or a tuple is.
- **Adding a tile's sprite puts it in the editor's palette by itself,** and the editor refuses a level whose
  tile has a detail it doesn't know (kindly: "has name, which the editor can't keep").

## What Chapter 8 holds, and why each thing sits where it does

One recommendation each, in plain words (M5 decision 7's way: the designer is past the Python he knows). Say so if
any reads wrong.

| Thing | Where | Why there |
|---|---|---|
| A **list**: several values in a row under one name, written `[1, 2, 3]`; `for` over it | 8.1 | The smallest idea first. The player has held lists since Chapter 6 without the word: `split()` hands one back. Everything that worked on text works on a list: `[0]`, `[-1]`, `len()`, `in`, `for` |
| A **tuple** (a pair): values that belong together and never change, written `(3, 2)`; **unpacking** (`x, y = pair`: one name for each value) | 8.2 | `knight.position` has been a pair since Chapter 2. A pair is what makes a list worth having: a list of places, a list of jumps |
| A function that hands back two things at once | an aside in 8.2's lesson | It is a tuple, and it ties the chapter to Chapter 7. No level forces it |
| `pick_up()`, `inventory`, `in` and `len()` on a list | 8.3 | The satchel is a list the *game* fills. Asking what is in it is the lesson |
| **`append`**: adding to a list of your own, one item at a time | 8.4 | Until here every list was handed over. Now the player builds one, from an empty `[]` |
| "`append` changes the list and hands back nothing" | one sentence and a `run error` snippet in 8.4's lesson | The most common slip with lists: keeping what `append` hands back, which is `None` |
| "Two names can be the same list" (a list given to a function is the same list inside it) | one sentence in 8.4's lesson | True, and it surprises people, but no level here needs it. The variables panel shows it happen |
| **`sorted()`, `min()`, `max()`** | 8.5 | They take a whole list and answer about all of it. `sorted()` hands back a new list and leaves the old one alone |
| `sum()` | one sentence in 8.5's lesson | The same family. 7.5 made the player add up by hand; this is the built-in for it |
| "`sorted()` on pairs orders by the first of each pair" | one sentence in 8.5's lesson | Useful and true; the mastery may lean on it |
| Slicing a list (`route[1:3]`) | one sentence in 8.1's lesson | It works exactly as on text (Chapter 6). No level needs it |
| `.sort()`, `.remove()`, `.pop()`, `.index()`, `.insert()`, `.reverse()` | not taught, not banned | One way to change a list (`append`) and one way to order it (`sorted()`) is enough for a first chapter. They are real Python and work; their mistakes get kind messages |
| Largest first (`sorted(..., reverse=True)`) | not here: Chapter 11 | It needs a keyword argument (M5 decision 7 puts those in Chapter 11). Levels ask for smallest first, or for the largest with `max()` |
| `enumerate`, `zip`, list comprehensions, lists inside lists | not here: Chapter 11 | Pairs inside a list are as deep as this chapter goes |
| `knight.jump(*pair)` (a star that spreads a pair into two arguments) | not taught: Chapter 15 | It works today and nothing is said. Unpacking is the lesson |
| `knight.look(dx, dy)` | still not used | It will name a key and a door, but no level here needs to look before it jumps |

## Decisions *(Designer, 2026-11-19: all ten as recommended)*

| # | Decided |
|---|---|
| 1 | **A key's name is text or a number, one kind per level:** named keys in 8.3, numbered keys in 8.5 and 8.6 |
| 2 | **A door doesn't use up its key.** The satchel only grows |
| 3 | **A door without its key, and `pick_up()` with nothing there, are errors at that line** |
| 4 | **`knight.inventory` is a copy each time,** with a note before the run when the code changes it in place |
| 5 | **The data comes from the examiner's lists, runes and the satchel.** No `knight.keys()`, no runes holding a list |
| 6 | **No doors that check the order of gathering.** A guard asks for the order in 8.5. This replaces M5's outline |
| 7 | **The satchel shows by the board only;** where and how is picked from the design sheet |
| 8 | **Keys and doors stay out of the editor's palette until M5.5** |
| 9 | **A "Kinds of value" section in the Codex:** `list`, `tuple`, and the Pawn tier's kinds filled in |
| 10 | **Titles and copy** as written |

The options stay below as the record of what wasn't chosen. 1 to 8 are about the game; 9 and 10 are about scope
and copy.

### 1. What a key is called

A key has a name, drawn on it, and the name is what lands in `knight.inventory`.

- **A. Text or a number, one kind per level** *(Recommended)*: named keys in 8.3 (`"brass"`, `"iron"`), so
  `"brass" in knight.inventory` reads like English; numbered keys in 8.5 and the mastery, where the keys are put
  in order, because "smallest first" is plainer with numbers than with the alphabet.
- B. Always text. Putting text in order is alphabetical, which works, but `min()` of names is a stranger idea.
- C. Always a number. One kind of thing everywhere, and a satchel of `[3, 7]` is less of a satchel.

### 2. Does a door use up its key?

- **A. No** *(Recommended)*: the key stays in the satchel and the door stays open for as long as it is carried.
  The satchel only grows, so the list the player reads never changes behind their back.
- B. Yes: the key leaves the satchel as the knight lands. It shows a list shrinking, and brings `remove` and
  "one key, two doors" puzzles, which are more than this chapter teaches.

### 3. A door without its key, and `pick_up()` with nothing there

- **A. An error at that line, both** *(Recommended)*: "The door on e5 is locked: it wants the brass key." It is
  what a guard's locked gate does to a knight today, and what `bridge()` does with no plank. The run stops, the
  line is marked, nothing is lost.
- B. A lost run for the door (the knight bounces into the chasm), an error for `pick_up()`.
- C. `pick_up()` on an empty stone does nothing and says nothing. Shorter code on boards where a stone may or may
  not hold a key, but a slip passes silently, and the player finds out three lines later.

### 4. What happens when code tries to change `knight.inventory`

The satchel has to be a real list (so `len`, `in`, `for`, `sorted` are the real thing) and the game has to stay
the judge (so writing a key into it must not open a door).

- **A. A copy each time, and a warning** *(Recommended)*: every time the code asks for `knight.inventory` it gets
  a fresh, ordinary list of what is carried. The player can do anything to that copy; it changes nothing on the
  board. If the code tries to change the satchel directly (`knight.inventory.append(...)`), a note before the run
  says so: "Your knight's satchel fills only with `pick_up()`. Changing `knight.inventory` changes a copy."
- B. A list that refuses changes with an error. Caught at the line, but it is no longer quite a list: its kind
  shows a name of its own, and a copy of it refuses `append` too, which fights 8.4.
- C. A tuple. Safe, but then it isn't a list: comparing it with one is false.

### 5. Where the data comes from

Lists are only worth learning if there is data the code couldn't have known ahead (pedagogy rule 10).

- **A. From what the game already has, widened** *(Recommended)*: the **examiner** hands lists and pairs to the
  player's function (8.1, 8.2, the mastery); **runes** hold numbers, as in Chapter 7 (8.4); the **satchel** is
  filled by keys that differ between boards (8.3, 8.5). No new ability to learn besides `pick_up()` and
  `inventory`.
- B. Also `knight.keys()`: an ability that hands back where every key lies, as a list of pairs. It makes boards
  the player's own program can explore, at the cost of one more ability, and of boards laid out so that every
  key is one jump from somewhere obvious (rule 16).
- C. Also runes that hold a list. A route "written down" on the board, read with `read()`. Cheap to build, but
  `read()` would hand back text on some runes and a list on others.

B and C can be added later without undoing A.

### 6. Doors that want their keys gathered in order

M5's outline had them. The trouble: to gather keys in an order, the code has to know each key's number and place
*before* touching it, so the level needs decision 5's option B, and a door that checks silently needs somewhere
to say what order it wants.

- **A. Not in M5.3: a guard asks for the order instead** *(Recommended)*. In 8.5 the knight gathers numbered keys
  in whatever order the path gives them, and the guards at the gate want to hear about the satchel: the smallest
  key, all of them in order. The putting-in-order is the code's work, the answer differs on every board, and no
  new kind of door is needed.
- B. A door that opens only if the satchel was filled smallest first, with `knight.keys()` handing over numbers
  and places. A stronger picture, a much bigger build (the ability, the ordered door, its drawing, its words),
  and a harder level than a first meeting with `sorted()` should be.

### 7. Where the satchel shows

- **A. By the board only** *(Recommended)*: a strip that fills as keys are picked up, drawn from the same state
  as the board, so it is right at every step. The design sheet shows where (under the board, or beside it) and
  how a key looks inside it. The variables panel shows `knight.inventory` only when the code gives it a name, as
  for any value.
- B. Also in the knight's row of the variables panel ("knight at c3, carrying ['brass']").

### 8. The editor until M5.5

- **A. Keys and doors stay out of the palette** *(Recommended)*, as M5 decision 9 says: the editor learns the
  whole tier once, in M5.5. Opening a Chapter 8 level in it is refused in a sentence, as today.
- B. In the palette now, with a field for the name. The editor's code is opened twice.

### 9. The Codex: lists and tuples themselves

`append`, `sorted`, `min`, `max` and `sum` become entries like `len` and `split` (no decision). But "what is a
list?" is neither an ability, a built-in nor a keyword.

- **A. A new section, "Kinds of value"** *(Recommended)*, with `list` and `tuple`, and the Pawn tier's kinds
  filled in as the keywords were in M5.2: text (`str`), whole numbers (`int`), `True` and `False` (`bool`),
  `None`. Each says how it is written, what it is for, and what works on it. `help([])` answers with the entry.
- B. `list` and `tuple` only.
- C. No section: what a list is stays in the lessons.

### 10. Titles and copy

The Satchel: **8.1 A List of Jumps · 8.2 Pairs · 8.3 The Satchel · 8.4 Gathering · 8.5 In Order · 8.6 The Vault**
(mastery). The examiner keeps its voice; the guards keep theirs. Briefs are with each level below.
*(Recommended: as written.)*

## The design sheet *(first, before any of it goes into the game)*

`design/satchel.html`, on the real board, both themes, as M5.2's sheet was. Nothing in the game changes until the
designer has picked.

1. **The key** on its stone: named and numbered (decision 1), and the stone after the key is taken.
2. **The door:** locked; open because its key is carried; how it says which key it wants.
3. **The satchel** (decision 7): two or three places and shapes; empty, with two keys, with eight.
4. **The two board shapes of the chapter** (below): a ladder and a hub, drawn as stones over the chasm.
5. **The examiner with a list:** its calls before the run and the buttons above the board when a call is as long
   as `climb([1, -1, -1, 1, 1])`; the result table when the answer wanted is a list.

## The levels

One chapter, six levels. The stones-over-a-chasm boards of Chapter 7 carry on (M5.2 decision 1). Two shapes are
new, and both keep the knight's geometry out of the puzzle (pedagogy rule 16):

- **A ladder:** a climb in which every jump goes the same distance up and one step to the left or the right.
  Which side is the only thing that changes, so a route up a ladder is a list of sides.
- **A hub:** a stone with other stones one jump away from it. Anything on those stones can be fetched in any
  order: out, and back.

Every level's abilities include `jump`; nothing else unless listed.

| Level | Trains | What makes it necessary |
|---|---|---|
| 8.1 A List of Jumps | a list, `for` over it | The examiner hands a different route on every board, and they differ in length |
| 8.2 Pairs | tuples, unpacking, `position` | The examiner hands the stones to visit as places, not jumps; each jump has to be worked out from two places |
| 8.3 The Satchel | `pick_up()`, `inventory`, `in` | Which keys a board holds differs, and only the doors that match will open |
| 8.4 Gathering | `append`, an empty list | The second half of each board has to be ridden with numbers read in the first half; they differ on every board, and there are too many for a name each |
| 8.5 In Order | `sorted()`, `min()`, `max()` | Guards ask about the satchel's keys, whose numbers differ on every board |
| 8.6 The Vault *(mastery, optional)* | all of it, to par | The examiner runs one behaviour on several boards |

### 8.1 · A List of Jumps

- **Trains:** a list as a value: written with square brackets, one name for the whole row; `for` over it, one
  item per lap; that the code doesn't need to know how long it is.
- **Brief:** "The examiner has written each climb down as a list: one number for every jump, 1 for a step to the
  right, -1 for a step to the left. It will call your spell `climb` with the list. Ride it."
- **The examiner calls** `climb(route)` on four boards, with lists of different lengths (three to five numbers).
- **Boards:** four ladders, each as tall as its list. The calls, lists and all, are shown before the run.
- **Rules:** the program only defines (M5.2 decision 4). **Par:** the spell at its shortest.
- **Must fail:** the items asked for one by one by their place (right for one length; on a shorter list an index
  past the end, with its new friendly message); a count of laps typed in.
- **Lesson:** a list made and printed, an item taken by its place, `len()`; a `for` over a list; "you have held
  one before: `split()`"; a `run error` snippet with an index past the end. One snippet is the examiner handing
  a list to a function on the lesson's board.

### 8.2 · Pairs

- **Trains:** a tuple: two values that travel together; unpacking it into two names; `knight.position` as a
  pair; a list of pairs; that a tuple can't be changed.
- **Brief:** "This time the examiner names the stones, not the jumps: a list of places, each an (x, y) pair.
  Your knight knows where it stands. Work out each jump."
- **The examiner calls** `visit(stops)` on four boards. Every stop is one jump from the stop before it (the first
  from the start), and the knight has to end on the last.
- **Boards:** four winding paths of stones of different lengths; the last stop is the goal.
- **Abilities:** `jump`, `position`. **Rules:** the program only defines. **Par:** the spell at its shortest.
- **Must fail:** a place handed to `jump()` as if it were a jump (not a knight's move, or the wrong stone); the
  pair itself handed to `jump()` (the kind message that exists); one board's jumps typed in.
- **Lesson:** a pair made, unpacked, and its items taken by place; `knight.position` printed and unpacked; a
  `for` over a list of pairs with two names; a `run error` snippet changing a tuple's item; a function that
  hands back two values (the aside).

### 8.3 · The Satchel

- **Trains:** `knight.pick_up()`; `knight.inventory`, a list the game fills; `in` to ask whether something is in
  a list; `len()` on it.
- **Brief:** "Two keys lie by the road, and further on it forks: a brass door one way, an iron door the other.
  Your knight can't know which keys it will find. Its satchel can."
- **Board:** driven by the player's own program, on three boards. The path passes two stones, each one jump off
  the road, with a key on each (out, pick up, and back); then it forks to two doors side by side and joins
  again before the goal. The keys are two of three kinds (brass, iron, bone), a different two on every board, so
  that on each board at least one door opens and no single door opens on all three.
- **Abilities:** `jump`, `pick_up`, `inventory`. **Rules:** none. **Par:** the route with one question asked of
  the satchel.
- **Must fail:** always taking the same door (a locked door on one board: an error at that line, decision 3);
  `pick_up()` on a stone with no key; a key written into the satchel by hand (decision 4's note, and the door
  stays shut).
- **Lesson** (on its own small board): a key picked up and the satchel printed before and after; `in` and
  `len()` on it; a door opened; a `run error` snippet landing on a door without its key, and one picking up
  where there is nothing (pedagogy rule 12).

### 8.4 · Gathering

- **Trains:** building a list: starting from an empty one, `append` adding one item to its end; then using the
  list that was built.
- **Brief:** "The first stones each carry a number. The ladder beyond them has no signs at all: the numbers you
  passed are its route. Remember every one."
- **Board:** driven by the player's own program, on three boards. A staircase of four rune stones (even jumps,
  each rune 1 or -1, different on every board), then a ladder of four jumps whose sides are those four numbers,
  in the order they were read.
- **Abilities:** `jump`, `read`. **Rules:** a line limit that four separate names do not fit. **Par:** the line
  limit, or one under.
- **Must fail:** one board's route typed in; a name for each number (too many lines); keeping what `append`
  hands back (an error, with the message that replaces the rune advice); the runes' text used as numbers without
  `int()` (Chapter 6).
- **Lesson:** an empty list, three `append`s and the list printed after each (the variables panel shows it
  grow); a list built in a loop; a `run error` snippet keeping what `append` hands back; one sentence on a list
  given to a function being the same list.

### 8.5 · In Order

- **Trains:** `sorted()`: a new list in order, the old one untouched; `min()` and `max()`; `sum()` in a sentence.
- **Brief:** "The keys of the vault are numbered. Two guards stand before it. The first asks for your smallest
  key. The second wants to hear all of them, smallest first."
- **Board:** driven by the player's own program, on three boards. A path with four numbered keys, each one jump
  off the road, the numbers different on every board and in no order; then two guards' gates in a row (heard, as
  in 7.5, from the stone before each), and the goal.
- **Abilities:** `jump`, `pick_up`, `inventory`. **Rules:** none. **Par:** the route, and one line for each
  guard.
- **Must fail:** one board's answers typed in; the satchel recited as it was filled; the numbers printed one per
  line or as text of the player's own making (the guard wants the list as Python prints it, and says so).
- **Lesson:** `sorted()` on a list of numbers, and the first list printed again, unchanged; `min()` and `max()`;
  `sorted()` on a list of pairs; a `run error` snippet with `max()` of an empty list.

### 8.6 · The Vault *(mastery, optional)*

- **Practises:** everything in the chapter, to par. It teaches nothing new (pedagogy rule 15): `for` over a list
  (8.1); pairs, unpacking and `position` (8.2); `pick_up()`, the satchel, a stone one jump off the road, out and
  back (8.3); `sorted()` (8.5); `def`, parameters, `return` and a helper function (Chapter 7).
- **Brief:** "The examiner's last test of the tier: a hub, keys scattered around it, and a list of where they
  lie. Fetch every one, and hand them back in order."
- **The examiner calls** one function on four boards with a list of places (pairs), a different number of them
  on each. The knight has to end back on the hub, carrying every key, and the function hands back the keys'
  numbers, smallest first, as a list.
- **Boards:** four hubs, each with as many keys around it as its call lists.
- **Abilities:** `jump`, `position`, `pick_up`, `inventory`. **Rules:** the program only defines. **Par:** tight.
- **Must fail:** one board's answer typed in; the satchel handed back as it was filled; a tuple handed back where
  a list is wanted ("the right idea in the wrong kind of thing").

**Pars and line limits are provisional** until the references are written blind. The generator's probe (counts
only) confirms every "must fail" and "what makes it necessary" above before QA1, and what it finds is recorded in
"As built".

## Level content *(the orchestrator's, written blind)*

A new generator, `scripts/gen_satchel.py`, writes `levels/ch08/`, `solutions/ch08/` (a reference, the wrong
attempts and a note for each level) and `lessons/ch08/`, and prints only counts. It lays out the ladders, paths
and hubs itself, builds each reference from them, and runs every reference and every wrong attempt on the real
engine. Hints follow the three tiers (a nudge; the mechanic in words; its code). `docs/Codex.md` gains the
chapter's entries.

## The new error messages: the words *(for packages M5.3-E1 and E2, read by the designer at QA1)*

In the voice the error cards already have. Where the engine can find the numbers it names them; where it can't,
the sentence without them stands.

| The mistake | What the card says |
|---|---|
| Keeping what `.append()` or `.sort()` hands back, then using it | `.append()` changes the list itself and hands back nothing (None). Call it on a line of its own, and keep using the list's own name. |
| An index past the end | That list has 3 items, so the last one is at `[2]`: places count from 0. There is nothing at `[3]`. |
| The same on a tuple | The same sentence, saying "tuple". |
| Unpacking the wrong number | There are 2 names on the left of the `=` and 3 values on the right. Unpacking needs exactly one name for each value. |
| Unpacking something that isn't a row of values | 5 is a whole number (an int): there is nothing inside it to hand out to `x` and `y`. Unpacking needs a tuple or a list. |
| Changing a tuple's item | A tuple can't be changed once it is made. Build a new one, or use a list if the values have to change. |
| Putting text and numbers in order together | Python can't put text and numbers in one order: it has no rule for whether 'a' comes before 3. |
| `remove` or `index` of something that isn't there | That value isn't in the list. Ask first with `in`. |
| `max()` or `min()` of an empty list | `max()` was handed an empty list, so there is no largest item to hand back. |
| A place that isn't a whole number (`a[1.0]`, `a['1']`) | A place in a list is a whole number, like `[0]`. 1.0 is a number with a decimal point (a float). |
| `list(5)` | `list()` makes a list out of something that has items in it, like text or a tuple. 5 is a whole number (an int). |
| `pick_up()` with nothing there | There is nothing to pick up on c3. |
| A door without its key | The door on e5 is locked: it wants the brass key, and your satchel holds ['iron']. |

## How it is built

**The contract, committed before any package** (names only; an Opus lead writes it as stubs and types). Its
first job is to finish what the investigation left open, on scratch copies: `inventory` as a copy with the
warning, run on the real engine; the examiner called with a list, a list of pairs and a list wanted back; where
`pick_up` and `inventory` have to be named for autocomplete, hover help and the lesson boards.

- **The level file,** so levels stay data:
  ```yaml
  legend:
    K: {tile: key, name: brass}      # a name is text or a whole number; one kind per level (decision 1)
    D: {tile: door, key: brass}
  examine:
    function: climb
    calls:
      - {args: [[1, -1, 1]], board: 1}                       # a list is a list
      - {args: [[[2, 1], [3, 3]]], board: 2, returns: [4, 9]}  # a list inside a list is a pair (a tuple)
  ```
  The checker refuses a door whose key is on no board of the level, two kinds of name in one level, and a
  key or a door together with what the search can't yet judge.
- **Engine:** `Tile.KEY`, `Tile.DOOR`; `Board.keys`, `Board.doors`; `World.inventory`, `World.pick_up()`;
  `PickUpError`, `DoorLockedError`; `Knight.pick_up()`, `Knight.inventory` (a copy, decision 4), on the knight
  only (the pawn can list them in M5.5: the world's side is the same for any piece). `look()` names a key and a
  door. A door is left out of the squares a knight may land on until its key is carried.
- **State and events:** `state["inventory"]` on levels with keys (the names, in the order picked up); a key that
  was taken goes in `collected`, as a plank does; an event for the pick-up with its sentence. `describe()` lists
  keys and doors and gains their sentences for the Challenge panel.
- **The examiner:** arguments and wanted answers may be lists; a list inside a list is a pair; `returns: {tuple:
  [...]}` when a tuple itself is wanted. Judging stays "the same kind of thing and equal". A lesson snippet the
  examiner calls may hand a list.
- **A note before the run** when the code changes `knight.inventory` in place (decision 4).
- **The search:** a deadline even when nobody sets one, routes kept as links to the step before instead of
  copies, and a new status that declines a level with keys or doors in a sentence, as it declines an examiner
  level. M5.5 teaches it the jump, the keys and the doors.
- **The Codex:** `sorted`, `min`, `max`, `sum` as built-ins; `list.append` beside the text methods, each method
  knowing what it belongs to; a kind `value` for decision 9.
- `protocol.ts` mirrors all of it: the two tile kinds, `inventory` in the state, the event, the level's keys and
  doors.

**Names:** the examiner's functions are never named like a built-in (M5.2's rule): `climb`, `visit`, not `sorted`.
