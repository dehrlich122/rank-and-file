# M3 · Chapters 2–6, the Pawn tier: plan, decisions and spec

M3 completes the Pawn tier (`DESIGN.md` §4 and §7). It's mostly level design,
so it runs slower than M2, with more checkpoints for the designer.

## How M3 runs *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*

- **Structure:** a chapter-level spec for Chapters 2–6 first (this document).
  It settles every question that reaches across chapters. Then one chapter
  per sub-milestone, starting with Chapter 2. Each gets its own branch, PR,
  play-test and QA rounds.
- **Oversight:** the designer approves a spec for every level before it's
  built, then play-tests the finished chapter.
- **Spoilers:** the designer reviews concepts, maps, rules, par and lessons.
  Solutions, naive attempts, hints and solution notes are written blind and
  verified automatically (`engine/tests/test_levels.py`). Hints are judged
  in play.

After M3 come M4 (the level editor and the Pawn tier's mastery set) and M4.x
(free play with a skins gallery). `DESIGN.md` §7 has the whole roadmap.

Inside each chapter sub-milestone:

1. Engine features first (if any), with tests.
2. **Checkpoint 1:** a level spec for each level, approved before anything is
   built. It gives the id and title, what the level trains, the board
   mechanic, a map sketch, abilities, rules, par and cases. It never gives
   solution text.
3. Build the levels, lessons, and the blind files.
4. **Checkpoint 2:** a PR with a manual test list, QA rounds, then merge.

## Cross-chapter decisions

Settled here before any chapter is built, because each one shapes more than
one chapter.

- **Chapter 2 makes variables and arithmetic necessary** *(Designer,
  2026-09-26)*:
  - Early levels put the numbers on signposts, and you do the arithmetic in
    code with variables.
  - Later levels add a counting ability (e.g. how many squares to the next
    wall). Its answer differs between a few boards, all shown before you
    run, so the arithmetic has to be in the code.
- **Chapter 3 forces loops with line limits and clockwork obstacles**
  *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*:
  - Long corridors under a line limit (as in the brief), with waypoints
    along them.
  - Then levels with a clockwork obstacle that moves the first time each
    line runs. Copying lines out by hand lets it catch you; a loop doesn't.
- **Nested loops come at the end of Chapter 3** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*,
  once `for` is solid: a staircase or a square route, as the last level or
  the mastery challenge.
- **Chapter size** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*:
  - **5 levels plus 1 mastery challenge per chapter.** The mastery challenge
    is harder, focused on par, and optional.
  - **At the end of each promotion** (each tier: Pawn, Knight, …), a set of
    optional mastery challenges covers the whole tier's concepts, in
    different ways.
  - The Pawn tier's set comes in **M4, after the level editor**, which will
    make those levels easier to author.
- **Chapter 4 is split in two** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*:
  - Chapter 4, "Eyes Open", covers booleans, comparisons and
    `if/elif/else`.
  - Chapter 5 covers `while`.
  - Runes becomes Chapter 6, so the Pawn tier has six chapters. Every later
    chapter in `DESIGN.md` §4 moves up by one (Spellbook becomes Chapter 7,
    and so on).
- **The counting ability reports the free squares ahead** *(Designer,
  2026-09-26)*: how many squares the pawn can move before something blocks
  it. Its name is settled in Chapter 2's level specs.
- **Unplaced concepts go where each fits** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*:
  - truthiness and `None` with conditions (Chapter 4)
  - `break` and `continue` with `while` (Chapter 5)
  - `in` with strings (Chapter 6), then lists later
- **Enemy chess pieces appear from Chapter 4** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*. The
  squares they attack are deadly, using real chess moves. They return more
  often as the player's own piece is promoted.

## Obstacles and threats: the toolkit

### The designer's ideas *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*

- **Moving obstacles,** some of them an instant loss if they reach you.
- **Patrols** that follow a set path, and **chasers** that follow the pawn.
  Each moves when triggered: by a line of code running, or by several lines
  running.
- **Waypoints** ("progress gates"): squares you must pass over at some point
  before reaching the goal. You don't have to stop on them, just cross them
  during the run.

### More ideas *(Claude, proposed)*

Each one is listed with the Python idea it would make necessary, which
decides where it first appears:

| Element | What it does | What it teaches | Could first appear |
|---|---|---|---|
| Timed gate | Open only on every Nth tick | `%` and arithmetic | Chapter 2 |
| Guard who wants a number | Like the gate guard, but asks for a computed answer ("the sum of the signs") | f-strings | Chapter 2 |
| Collectibles | Gems to pick up; "collect 5" objectives | counters in variables | Chapter 2 |
| Conveyor arrows | Push the pawn one square when stepped on | predicting a sequence | Chapter 3 |
| Pits and water | Stepping in is an instant loss | `if` and `look()` | Chapter 4 |
| Pressure plates, switches | Open or close walls while held, or on a toggle | booleans | Chapter 4 |
| Ice | You slide until you hit something | `while` loops | Chapter 4 |
| Sentries | Watch a line; being seen is an instant loss | comparisons | Chapter 4 |
| Fog | Hides squares until the pawn senses them | `sense()` / `look()` | Chapter 4 |
| Enemy chess pieces | Squares they attack are deadly, using real chess moves | geometry, conditions | Chapter 4, then the higher tiers |
| Crumbling floor | Each square can be crossed only once | sets | Chapter 8 |
| Keys and locked doors | Doors need their key | lists, dicts | Chapters 7–8 (as in the brief) |
| Teleporter pairs | Step in one, come out of its twin | dicts | Chapter 8 |
| Movable blocks | Push them to clear a path | planning, functions | Chapter 6 |
| Traps | Raise an exception when stepped on | `try/except` | Chapter 9 (as in the brief) |

### How the toolkit works *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*

- **The clock is set per obstacle.**
  - Most obstacles move once per pawn action (a move or a turn), like chess:
    you move, then they move.
  - Special "clockwork" obstacles follow the code instead. They move each
    time a line runs, or the first time each line runs (which rewards
    loops).
  - Each level says which kind every obstacle is.
- **Everything is shown before you run.** Patrol routes, chaser rules,
  attacked squares, waypoints and timings are drawn on the board. Only
  elements whose whole point is being hidden (fog, traps) stay hidden.
- **Losing is a final outcome.**
  - Being caught, or falling in a pit, stops the run with its own clear
    outcome (e.g. "Caught by the patrol on d4"). It can be replayed like any
    run.
  - It isn't a Python error the code could catch. Chapter 9's traps are the
    kind that raise exceptions, so `try/except` gets its own mechanic.
- **The toolkit comes first.** A new sub-milestone, M3.1, builds the engine
  for the clock, moving obstacles, instant losses and waypoints. It comes
  with practice levels in the Testing ground, so the designer can play each
  mechanic before deciding where it goes in the chapters.
- **M3.1 builds** *(Designer, 2026-09-26)*:
  - patrols and chasers, with both clock kinds
  - waypoints
  - pits and other instant-loss squares
  - Chapter 2's helpers: timed gates, guards who want a computed answer,
    and collectibles

  The rest of the table waits for the chapters and tiers that need it.

## Chapter outlines *(Claude, proposed; for the designer's approval)*

Each chapter has 5 levels plus 1 optional mastery challenge. The lines below
say what each level teaches and its board mechanic, never how to solve it.
Each chapter's level specs (checkpoint 1) turn these lines into maps, rules
and par, and can change them.

### M3.1 · The toolkit (Testing ground)

- **Engine:**
  - **The clock,** set per obstacle: per pawn action, per line of code, or
    the first time each line runs.
  - **Moving obstacles:** patrols on a drawn route, and chasers with a stated
    rule.
  - **A new "lost" outcome** for being caught or stepping into a pit.
  - **Waypoints.**
  - **Timed gates:** open on every Nth tick.
  - **Guards who want a computed answer.**
  - **Collectibles.**
- **Board and UI:**
  - routes, chase rules, timings and waypoints drawn before the run
  - the new outcome on the card
  - playback of moving obstacles (their positions join the world state that
    every event carries)
- **Testing ground:** one short practice level per mechanic, so the designer
  can play each one before it's placed in a chapter.

### Chapter 2 · Counting Steps: variables, ints, arithmetic, f-strings

- **Forcing:** signposts give the numbers at first. Later, the counting
  ability gives numbers that differ between a few boards, all shown before
  the run.
- **New engine work:**
  - the counting ability (the free squares ahead)
  - previews of a level's other boards before the run: the variants shown up
    front, as decided in QA-016
- **Levels:**
  1. **Naming a number:** a signpost's number stored in a variable and used
     more than once.
  2. **Doing the sums:** signposts give the parts; the distances are worked
     out in code.
  3. **Telling the guard:** a guard wants a computed answer (f-strings).
  4. **Counting on the board:** the counting ability, on boards of different
     lengths (integer division).
  5. **Keeping time:** a timed gate that opens on every Nth move (`%`).
  - **Mastery:** the counting ability, a timed gate and a guard together,
    across several boards, focused on par.
- **An idea for the level specs** (QA-022, QA-023): count gems in a variable
  as they're collected, then give the total to a guard who asks. A question
  guard is only worth having when its answer can't be known ahead of time
  (e.g. it differs between the level's boards). Otherwise it's a passphrase
  counted by eye. The Testing ground's The Toll is only a demo of the two
  mechanics.
- **Decided before the level specs** *(Designer, 2026-09-29)*:
  - **The counting ability is `pawn.squares_ahead()`.**
    - It returns how many squares the pawn can walk before something would
      stop it: a wall, the board's edge, a shut or locked gate. So
      `pawn.move(pawn.squares_ahead())` never bumps.
    - Counting costs no tick, like `at_goal()`.
    - It gets a Codex entry like every ability.
  - **New code rules force variables and sums on the signpost levels.**
    Both are checked with `ast`, like the existing rules.
    - 2.1: "each number may appear only once in your code", so the number is
      named and the name reused.
    - 2.2: "only numbers from the signposts may appear", so the sum happens
      in code.
    - Not chosen: line limits only, or no forcing.
  - **`wait()` takes an optional number of ticks.**
    - `pawn.wait(3)` waits 3 ticks, and `pawn.wait()` still waits 1, the
      same as `move()`.
    - This reverses M3.1's "`wait()` takes no number", because 2.5 needs a
      computed wait before loops arrive in Chapter 3.
    - Not chosen: moving the timed gate to Chapter 3, or keeping `wait()`
      as it is.
  - **A level's other boards show as tabs above the board,** before the run:
    "Board 1 · Board 2 · Board 3". Clicking one shows that board. After a
    run the same tabs show ✓ or ✗ and replay each board, as the ? squares
    do now.
    - Not chosen: previews in the Challenge panel, or both.
- **New engine and UI work,** from the decisions: `squares_ahead()`,
  `wait(ticks)`, the two number rules, and the board tabs.

### Chapter 3 · Marching Orders: `for`, `range()`, nested loops

- **Forcing:** line limits, waypoints, and clockwork obstacles that move the
  first time each line runs.
- **New engine work:** none expected beyond M3.1.
- **Levels:**
  1. **Repeat a step:** a long corridor under a tight line limit.
  2. **Repeat a pattern:** a staircase route with waypoints.
  3. **The loop's counter:** the loop variable and `range(start, stop, step)`.
     Collectibles are counted along the way.
  4. **Outpacing the clockwork:** an obstacle that moves the first time each
     line runs.
  5. **Loops inside loops:** a route whose repeating part itself repeats.
  - **Mastery:** clockwork, waypoints and nested loops, focused on par.

### Chapter 4 · Eyes Open: booleans, comparisons, `if/elif/else`

- **Forcing:** cases. The same level on a few boards (shown up front), or a
  goal on ? squares, where only a check of the board gets it right every
  time. Pits make a wrong guess an instant loss.
- **New engine work:**
  - `look()`: what's in the next square
  - enemy chess pieces: attacked squares drawn on the board
  - capturing is already built in M3.1: take an enemy diagonally forward
    (`capture_left()` / `capture_right()`); first used here, since checking
    the board first matters
  - pressure plates or switches, if a level needs them
- **Levels:**
  1. **True or false:** `at_goal()` and `look()` answers, printed and
     compared.
  2. **If:** a pit that's there on some boards and not on others.
  3. **If/else:** the path bends one way on some boards, the other way on
     others.
  4. **Elif, and comparing numbers:** three possibilities. The counting
     ability's answers are compared (`<`, `>`, `==`), and a missing answer
     is `None` (truthiness).
  5. **Under attack:** an enemy rook or bishop whose attacked squares are
     deadly.
  - **Mastery:** conditions inside a `for` loop across several boards.
- **Decided before the level specs** *(Designer, 2026-10-01)*:
  - **`pawn.look()` gives back a word, or None.** It names what's on the
    square ahead: "wall", "pit", "gate", "rook" and so on. It gives back
    None when the square is empty, so `if pawn.look():` asks "is anything
    there?". That brings in None and truthiness.
    - Not chosen: always a word ("floor" for empty), or a True/False
      ability for each kind of thing.
  - **`pawn.look("left")` and `pawn.look("right")` see diagonally ahead**,
    the two squares a pawn captures on. Plain `look()` is straight ahead.
    - Not chosen: straight ahead only, or `can_capture_left()`-style
      checks.
  - **Enemy chess pieces are still rooks and bishops.** They stand still.
    Stepping onto a square one attacks (real chess lines, stopped by walls,
    closed gates and pieces) loses the run, and those squares are drawn on
    the board. A rook can be taken from the diagonal. A bishop attacks its
    diagonals, so it can't be approached that way and must be avoided.
    - Not chosen: rooks only, or pieces that also move.
  - **`and`, `or` and `not` are taught in Chapter 4**, with booleans.
    - Not chosen: Chapter 5 with `while`, or later.

### Chapter 5 · Keep Going: `while`, `break`, `continue`

- **Forcing:** a goal hidden on ? squares, chasers, and distances that vary,
  none of them solvable by a fixed count.
- **New engine work:** ice (slide until something stops you), if a level
  needs it.
- **Levels:**
  1. **Until you arrive:** a goal hidden on ? squares.
  2. **Until something changes:** conditions on the board; ice, if built.
  3. **Counting while walking:** a counter kept inside a `while` loop.
  4. **Stopping early, skipping ahead:** `break` and `continue`.
  5. **The chaser:** a chaser that follows each move, needing a loop that
     reacts every step.
  - **Mastery:** chasers and hidden goals together.

### Chapter 6 · Runes: strings, indexing, slicing, methods, `in`

- **Forcing:** rune tiles whose text differs between boards. The destination
  or passphrase has to be decoded in code.
- **New engine work:** rune tiles and `read()`.
- **Levels:**
  1. **Reading a rune:** `read()` and printing it; indexing single
     characters.
  2. **Slicing:** a number or direction cut out of a rune's text.
  3. **String methods:** a guard's passphrase decoded with methods such as
     `.upper()` and `.replace()`.
  4. **In:** choosing a path by whether a rune contains a word.
  5. **Decoding the destination:** text turned into numbers (`int()`) and
     moves.
  - **Mastery:** several runes across several boards, focused on par.

Then comes the promotion to Knight (Chapter 7 onwards, in a later
milestone). The Pawn tier's set of mastery challenges comes in M4.
