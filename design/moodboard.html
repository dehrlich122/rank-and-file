Mood board · round 2

After the first tiles, the direction moved from old video games to 80s cyberpunk and retro-futurism: Hotline Miami, Hackers, The Lawnmower Man and their kin. Each entry says what Rank & File takes from it and, as importantly, what it leaves behind. The brief's priorities still decide: where a reference pulls against readability, readability wins.

  • 1 readability is the product
  • 2 a coding game as much as a chess game
  • 3 enemies are corrupted code
  • 4 80s retro-futurism, in the chrome

The direction

chrome · title screen

Hotline Miami Dennaton Games, 2012

A neon-soaked Miami night: a sunset palette, heavy type with hard stacked shadows, a title screen that sways, and a VHS haze over everything.

Take: the sunset on the title screen and in the level-complete flourish, chrome lettering with a two-step offset shadow, a gently swaying logo, a VHS tracking band.

Leave: the violence, entirely. Also the screen that wobbles and colour-cycles during play: on our board nothing moves unless the code made it move, and pink stays hostile there.

Wikipedia
world · voice

Hackers film, 1995

Cyberspace as a glowing city of data, fluorescent type on black, and hackers who treat a computer as a playground.

Take: the board as a running system lit from inside; cyan, green and amber neon on near-black; the player as the one writing the program, in a playful chrome voice.

Leave: the rapid cuts and the text flying through space. Code and lessons sit still and stay plain.

Wikipedia
sprites

The Lawnmower Man film, 1992

Early computer graphics: virtual reality built from flat-shaded polygons, chrome and wireframe.

Take: the vector sprites: flat faces lit from one side with a thin lit edge; the pit as a wireframe funnel falling away.

Leave: the morphing bodies and the horror. Our figures are stable; only enemies glitch.

Wikipedia
corruption

Max Headroom television, 1985

A computer-generated TV host who stutters, skips and breaks up: a broken signal with a face.

Take: the language of corruption for enemies: a stuttered frame, an RGB split, a slice that slips sideways, in bursts.

Leave: the constant stutter. Bursts come every few seconds, and never touch code, text or the player's piece.

Wikipedia
board · player

Tron film, 1982

People and machines as glowing outlines on a dark grid. The world is a computer, and the player is a program inside it.

Take: the lit grid, the lit edge on every sprite, and the player as the program: clean, cyan, stable.

Leave: heavy bloom everywhere. Glow stays on edges, sprites and lines, in the dark theme only.

Wikipedia
HUD

Johnny Mnemonic film, 1995

Cyberspace interfaces: wireframe frames, brackets and floating panels around data.

Take: the HUD frame with corner brackets around the Lessons directory and the level's panels.

Leave: the gloom and the clutter of floating windows.

Wikipedia
world text

Blade Runner film, 1982

Neon signs as the only light in a dark city: the world speaks in lit lettering.

Take: runes and signs as the world's lit text, in phosphor green, the one place on the board where the world "talks".

Leave: the rain, the smog and the brown murk. Our squares stay clean.

Wikipedia
motion · ceremony

Akira film, 1988

Light trails that hang in the air behind a moving bike, and poster-bold type.

Take: a short light trail behind the moving piece (the motion kit, step 2) and bold type energy for the promotion ceremony.

Leave: red for anything but hostile, and all of the violence.

Wikipedia
title screen

Out Run and the synthwave sunset Sega, 1986, and after

A striped sun on the horizon and a perspective grid running to it.

Take: the title composition: logo, striped sun, scrolling grid, and now the enemy rank on the horizon with the hero below.

Leave: palm trees, and any of it inside the play area.

Wikipedia
logo

Kung Fury and Blood Dragon 2015 and 2013

Modern love letters to the 80s: chrome logos split by a horizon line, laser grids, VHS.

Take: the chrome logo: sky above the horizon line, sunset below it, a hard shadow under it.

Leave: the parody and the excess. The game's own text is never a joke at the learner's expense.

Kung Fury · Blood Dragon
restraint

Drive film, 2011

Neon-pink titles over a night city, used sparingly, so each one lands.

Take: restraint. One big chrome moment at a time, over darkness, and quiet everywhere else.

Leave: the script lettering for anything someone has to read quickly.

Wikipedia
lines

Tempest and Battlezone Atari, 1981 and 1980

Vector arcade games: thin, bright lines on black, where the line itself is the picture.

Take: thin lit lines with a faint glow, for the board grid and its edge.

Leave: wireframe-only figures, which lose their body at small sizes; our vector sprites keep filled faces.

Tempest · Battlezone

Kept for readability

priority 1

Into the Breach Subset Games, 2018

Every threat is shown on the board before it happens, on the exact squares it will hit.

Take: telegraphing an attack on the squares themselves: our hatch for the squares a rook or bishop threatens.

Leave: the isometric view and the pale interface.

Wikipedia
priority 2

TIS-100 and EXAPUNKS Zachtronics, 2015 and 2018

Games where the code is the interface: line numbers, a step line and a machine you program.

Take: line numbers, the step line and coordinates treated as part of the HUD.

Leave: the dense panels and walls of manual text.

TIS-100 · EXAPUNKS

Dialled down after round 1

A Link to the Past, Shovel Knight, Hyper Light Drifter, Dead Cells, Loop Hero, PICO-8 and Hacknet shaped the first tiles' pixel sprites and terminal voice. Their lessons about clear silhouettes and limited palettes still hold, but they no longer set the look.
