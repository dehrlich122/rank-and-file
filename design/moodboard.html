Mood board

Fourteen references in twelve entries, each with what Rank & File takes from it and, as importantly, what it leaves behind. They are listed in the brief's priority order: readability first, then the coding-game feel, then enemies as corrupted code, then the 80s retro-futurism. Where a reference pulls against a higher priority, the entry says so.

  • 1 readability is the product
  • 2 a coding game as much as a chess game
  • 3 enemies are corrupted code
  • 4 80s retro-futurism, in the chrome
priority 1 · 2

The Legend of Zelda: A Link to the Past SNES, 1991

Chunky, readable sprites with a dark outline and a few tones of shading, drawn small enough to read at a glance, and a character that has personality in a two-frame idle.

Take: the 1px outline, 3–4 tone shading, and a two-frame idle for the hero. Every object has its own silhouette.

Leave: its busy, textured tiles. Our squares stay quiet; the art lives on them, not in them.

Wikipedia
priority 1 · 2

Shovel Knight Yacht Club Games, 2014

A modern game that looks like an 8-bit one: a limited palette, high contrast, and an armoured hero whose helmet alone tells you who it is.

Take: the armoured hero whose silhouette is the character. This is the model for a pawn and a knight that read as chess pieces.

Leave: the strict console-palette rules, and the side-scrolling layouts. We need modern, crisp pixel art, not a retro filter.

Wikipedia
priority 3 · 4

Hyper Light Drifter Heart Machine, 2016

Bright magenta, cyan and amber accents against a dark world, with a glitching, corrupted-data motif around its enemies and ruins.

Take: bright accents on near-black, and the idea that a corrupted thing flickers while a safe thing sits still.

Leave: the constant ambient particles and fog, which break our one-ambient-motion-per-square rule, and magenta as the world's main colour. Ours is for enemies only.

Wikipedia
priority 1

Dead Cells Motion Twin, 2018

Pixel art that stays readable at speed: strong silhouettes, clear colour families for friends and foes, and a brief flash when something is hit.

Take: a crisp silhouette that survives at a small size, and short, sharp state flashes rather than long effects.

Leave: the screen shake, particles and the pre-rendered 3D look. Playback has to stay calm so the code can be followed.

Wikipedia
priority 1

Loop Hero Four Quarters, 2021

A game played on tiles, with a restrained palette and icon-driven state. The board is always the main thing you look at.

Take: tile-first restraint: the tile says what it is, and nothing else competes with it.

Leave: the muted, desaturated palette and the small dense text. Ours is lit, and the text has to be easy.

Wikipedia
priority 1 · 3

Into the Breach Subset Games, 2018

Every threat is shown on the board before it happens, on the exact squares it will hit, and the whole game is built so you can read the state without effort.

Take: telegraphing an attack on the squares themselves. This is our hatch for the squares a rook or bishop threatens.

Leave: the isometric mechs and the pale blue interface.

Wikipedia
priority 2

TIS-100 and EXAPUNKS Zachtronics, 2015 and 2018

Games where the code is the interface: monospaced text, line numbers, terse manual-style wording and a screen that looks like a machine you are programming.

Take: treating line numbers, the step line and coordinates as part of the HUD, and the terse tone.

Leave: the dense multi-panel screens and the walls of manual text. Our teaching text stays short and plain.

TIS-100 · EXAPUNKS
priority 4

Tempest and Battlezone Atari, 1981 and 1980

Vector arcade games: thin, bright lines on black, where the line itself is the picture.

Take: thin lit lines with a faint glow, for the board grid and its edge.

Leave: wireframe-only characters (they lose their body at small sizes) and the flicker as visual noise.

Tempest · Battlezone
priority 4 · 2

Tron film, 1982

People and machines as glowing outlines on a dark grid. The world is a computer, and the player is a program inside it.

Take: the lit outline (the "neon vector" tile) and the idea of the player as the program, clean and lit.

Leave: the heavy bloom and circuit patterns everywhere. Glow stays on edges, sprites and lines, never on text.

Wikipedia
priority 4

Out Run and the synthwave title Sega, 1986, and after

A striped sun on the horizon and a perspective grid running to it: the picture most people mean by "80s".

Take: the title screen composition only: logo, sun, scrolling grid, a blinking cursor on the menu.

Leave: palm trees, pink-to-orange everywhere, and any of it inside the play area. This belongs to the chrome.

Wikipedia
priority 2

Hacknet Team Fractal Alligator, 2015

A game played in a terminal, in the terse voice of a command line.

Take: the voice for headings and folders: $ ls lessons/, short and flat.

Leave: the hacker jargon. A clever line that slows down understanding is a bug, so lessons, hints and errors stay plain.

Wikipedia
priority 1

PICO-8 Lexaloffle, 2015

A fantasy console with one small, fixed palette. Everything made for it looks like it belongs together.

Take: palette discipline: every sprite is drawn from a handful of named tones, so a new tier's art slots in.

Leave: the fixed 16 colours. Ours are tokens, so the light theme and the contrast check keep working.

Wikipedia
